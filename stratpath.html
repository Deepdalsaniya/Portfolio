Case Study · AI Product / Strategy

Turning faculty rubrics into feedback students trust, with a human still in the loop.

Business-school faculty spend hours giving each student rubric-grounded feedback, and it still comes out slow and uneven. StratPath AI is the Bedrock/RAG platform I co-founded to fix that: it grounds every response in the professor's own rubric and materials, gates it behind acceptance criteria and human review, and proves it works with telemetry. I owned product strategy, requirements, and controls, taking it from zero to 1,000+ monthly users and $210K raised.

01 · THE PROBLEM

Good feedback doesn't scale, and generic AI feedback isn't trusted.

Business-school assessment has a structural bottleneck: the feedback that actually helps a student is tied to a specific rubric, a specific assignment, and a specific instructor's intent, and producing it by hand is slow and inconsistent across sections and graders. The obvious shortcut, a general-purpose chatbot, fails the moment a professor reads the output: it invents criteria, praises what the rubric penalizes, and can't cite where a judgment came from. Faculty won't put their name behind feedback they can't trace, and students can tell when it's boilerplate.

So the real requirement was never "generate feedback." It was generate feedback a professor is willing to sign — grounded in their materials, consistent with their rubric, and reviewable before it reaches a student.

02 · FROM FACULTY NEEDS TO REQUIREMENTS

I sat with the people who owned the rubric.

Professors supplied the rubrics, course materials, and lecture recordings. My job was to turn what they meant by "good feedback" into something a system could execute and a human could check.

i.

Grounding sources

Rubrics, assignment briefs, course readings, and lecture recordings became the retrieval corpus, so every response cites the instructor's own material, not the open web.

ii.

Requirements

I translated faculty expectations into explicit product requirements: what a response must reference, what it must never claim, and where a human has to sign off.

iii.

Acceptance criteria

Each feedback type shipped with pass/fail criteria — rubric coverage, citation to source, and tone — that an output had to meet before it was allowed to surface.

03 · THE WORKFLOW

Retrieve, ground, check, then let a human decide.

A Bedrock/RAG pipeline produces rubric-grounded feedback; nothing reaches a student without clearing the criteria and, where it matters, a human-review gate.

What the telemetry was for

The 50,000+ daily events weren't a vanity dashboard. They were how we saw which feedback types faculty actually kept, which ones they overrode, and where the model was drifting off-rubric. Instrumenting adoption and override behavior is what let a small team run pilots responsibly instead of shipping blind.

Sustaining 97% user satisfaction while cutting manual review 90% only held because the human-review gate stayed on the path where the stakes justified it.

06 · WHAT THE DATA CHANGED

The findings moved the roadmap, not the other way around.

The telemetry didn't just report on the product; it re-ordered it. Where the data showed faculty consistently overriding a feedback type, that signaled the grounding or the acceptance criteria were wrong, and it moved up the backlog ahead of net-new features. Where adoption clustered, the rollout followed the evidence instead of the original plan. That is the loop I care about: instrument the AI, read the override behavior honestly, and let it decide what ships next.

The transferable pattern here is exactly what a regulated analytics team needs from AI: faculty owned the ground truth, the system stayed grounded in it, acceptance criteria and human review gated the output, and telemetry proved whether it was trusted in practice — then the roadmap answered to that evidence.

Figures are from StratPath's pilot period (Feb 2025 – Jan 2026): 1,000+ monthly users, $210K raised, and telemetry measured at 50,000+ events per day.

07 · LEARNINGS

Trust was the product; the model was a component.

  • Grounding beats fluency. Faculty didn't want a smarter writer; they wanted feedback they could trace to their own rubric and materials, which is why RAG over the instructor corpus mattered more than model choice.
  • The human-review gate is a feature, not a fallback. Keeping a person on the high-stakes path is what got faculty to adopt at all, and what kept satisfaction at 97% while manual effort fell.
  • Instrument overrides, not just usage. The most useful signal wasn't how often the tool was used, it was how often faculty corrected it — that's where the roadmap came from.
